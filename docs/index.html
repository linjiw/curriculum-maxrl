Research notes · curriculum learning × MaxRL

The estimator is the curriculum

In RL with verifiable rewards, rollout compute is wasted twice: on prompts the model has mastered (nothing left to learn) and on prompts it can't touch (every rollout fails — group estimators emit zero gradient). We measured it: under uniform sampling, historical logs showed 65–75% zero-weight groups, but the old counter mixed all-fail and all-pass groups. The statistic needs no new estimator machinery. We prove practical MaxRL's expected scalar coefficient mass is exactly 2·(pass@N − pass@1) — twice the probability of being solvable in N tries but not in one — and that formula supplies a curriculum proxy: sample by it to favor groups likely to emit practical MaxRL coefficient mass. An already-realized all-fail group has no practical MaxRL update, so selection alone cannot recover that group; where a verifier-valid goal rewrite exists, we recycle a failed rollout as a success for an achieved goal. Together they beat a true-pass-rate proportional-priority comparator on the toy — because that comparator only reallocates original-task groups, while recycling adds verifier-valid auxiliary targets.

The one-sentence pitch

Read the curriculum off the estimator's own algebra, and salvage what the estimator throws away. No hand-set band center or width: the rollout budget N sets the coefficient-mass band (peak pass rate ≈ ln N / N). Floor, concentration, decay, and task bins remain empirical; the practical drop-both estimator targets order N−1, while raw and always-retained-CV variants retain the paper's order-N result.

0 · Why this direction

MaxRL's weight-function view (below) shows the likelihood objective already pours gradient into hard prompts — an implicit curriculum. That raises a question with a non-obvious answer: if the objective already reweights toward hard prompts, is there anything left for a curriculum to do? Either curricula are redundant with likelihood-based RL (a useful negative result), or they do something gradient reweighting cannot (a useful positive one).

The answer is sharply the second, for a structural reason: weights act on prompts only after they are sampled, and only when at least one rollout succeeds. However w(p) is shaped, it cannot rescue a prompt whose group came back all-fail — the group is dropped in the practical implementation—and it cannot un-spend compute burned re-confirming mastered prompts. So the curriculum's real job is not "emphasize hard prompts" (the objective does that); it is to manage exactly the two regimes the estimator is blind to. That reframing — the estimator's blind spots define the curriculum's job description — is the origin of everything on this page.

Channel 1 → the teacher (waste avoidance)

Theorem 1 expresses the ML gradient through success-conditioned scores; the practical centered update also scores failures in live mixed groups but discards K=0 groups. Its expected scalar coefficient mass per prompt has a closed form — and that form, 2·(pass@N − pass@1), is a zone-of-proximal-development functional the estimator was computing implicitly all along. Sampling by it is the curriculum. A true-pass-rate proportional-priority comparator is a useful reference: allocation only redistributes original-task groups, and no optimal-sampler bound has been established.

Channel 2 → the recycler (signal creation)

If an all-fail group supplies no update, create a verified auxiliary success. A failed rollout is a verified success for the goal it actually reached; relabeling dead groups yields verifier-valid auxiliary updates. Equality to a fresh target update needs the law/moment conditions in Proposition 6. This changes what a sampled failure can yield in a way prompt selection alone cannot.

A third channel is the learner interaction: a curriculum changes the data stream, and the learner turns that stream into updates. One historical maze sweep showed the same teacher interacting in opposite directions with MaxRL and GRPO, but its score, counters, budgets, and AUC protocol were confounded. That observation is a hypothesis for a corrected factorial, not a general conclusion about GRPO curricula. Together: the teacher reallocates groups, hindsight adds auxiliary targets, and the learner converts those data into updates. The full evidence decomposition, regime map, and practitioner playbook are in EVIDENCE.md.

1 · How MaxRL works, in three steps

Step 1 — RL and maximum likelihood optimize different things. For a prompt x, let p be the model's probability of producing a verified-correct answer (its pass rate). Standard RL maximizes the expected reward E[p]; maximum likelihood maximizes E[log p]. Their gradients differ by one factor:

∇JRL = 𝔼x[ ∇p ]    vs.    ∇JML = 𝔼x[ 1p ∇p ]

That 1/p is the whole story: maximum likelihood pours learning signal into hard, low-pass-rate prompts, while plain RL treats every prompt equally. In differentiable supervised learning this is why cross-entropy works so well — but with sampled rollouts and a binary verifier you cannot compute ∇log p directly.

Step 2 — the Maclaurin bridge. The paper's key identity expands log p in failure events, turning the intractable objective into an infinite mixture of pass@k objectives:

log p = −Σk≥1 (1−p)kk   ⇒   ∇JML = Σk≥1 1k ∇pass@k

Plain RL keeps only the k=1 term — it is a first-order approximation of maximum likelihood. Truncating the series at order T gives a family of objectives that interpolates from RL (T=1) to ML (T→∞).

Step 3 — a one-line estimator. Sample N rollouts, count K successes. REINFORCE averages score functions over all N samples; MaxRL averages over the K successful ones (and skips the prompt when K=0). Theorem 2 of the paper: this simple change is unbiased for the truncated objective with T = N. The always-retained control-variate form is also order N, while practical Algorithm 1 drops both terms at K=0 and targets T = N−1. In the practical algorithm it is one line: normalize the advantage by the group's mean reward (instead of the std as in GRPO, or nothing as in RLOO).

2 · Where the implicit curriculum goes silent

The weight-function view is a population (infinite-sample) statement. With finite groups of N rollouts, three gaps open up — and they are precisely the gaps a data-level teacher can close:

Dead prompts (K = 0)

If p ≪ 1/N, all N rollouts fail with probability (1−p)N ≈ 1. The group is dropped: zero gradient, wasted generation compute. No choice of w(p) can put signal where the group dies. Historical maze logs recorded about 65% zero-weight groups under uniform sampling, but that counter mixed K=0 with K=N. It therefore cannot estimate all-fail waste; corrected runs log those cases separately.

Mastered prompts (p → 1)

∇p → 0, so rollouts are spent re-confirming what the model knows. GRPO's population coefficient curve turns upward and relatively upweights easy prompts; whether that harms a particular learner is an empirical interaction question.

Uniform rollout budgets

The paper uses a fixed N per prompt. But since the truncation order is tied to the group size (T = N for the raw and always-retained estimators; T = N−1 for the practical drop-both estimator), a larger group both raises the chance of a first success and changes the effective objective order. Variable N is therefore an objective change, not pure compute allocation.

A striking finite-sample fact we verified: at p = 0.01, N = 32, GRPO's population curve gives coefficient mass ≈ 0.199, but the realized expected coefficient mass is 0.100 — half — because 72% of groups are all-fail and contribute nothing. Population weight curves describe a limit no finite-group trainer lives in.

3 · Our proposal: read the curriculum off the estimator

Instead of importing a curriculum heuristic (difficulty bands, learning-progress bandits), we asked: how much scalar coefficient mass does practical MaxRL emit on a prompt, in expectation? Define the advantage mass of a group as the total magnitude of its advantage weights, Σ|w|. For MaxRL's variance-reduced estimator (wsucc = 1/K − 1/N, wfail = −1/N, group dropped at K=0), the algebra collapses to something exact and interpretable:

𝔼[ Σ|w| ] = 2·( pass@N(p) − pass@1(p) ) = 2·( (1−(1−p)N) − p )

The expected scalar coefficient mass on a prompt is twice the probability that it is solvable within N attempts but not within one. That is a compute-indexed formalization of the zone of proximal development, produced by the estimator itself. It vanishes at both ends — beyond-frontier prompts (group dies) and mastered prompts (no headroom) — and peaks at

p★ = 1 − N−1/(N−1) ≈ ln NN

so larger group sizes shift the coefficient-mass peak toward harder prompts. This proxy is not the gradient norm or a proof of optimal learning progress.

Watch it work: the frontier walk, animated

The teacher loop

  1. Track. Keep discounted Beta(α, β) pseudo-counts for each prompt's pass rate, updated from every group's observed successes (decay 0.7—the estimate's job is tracking the moving student, not remembering).
  2. Score. Draw p̃ from each pseudo-count distribution; compute u(p̃) = (1−(1−p̃)N) − p̃. Randomized draws encourage exploration of rarely-visited prompts for free.
  3. Sample. Draw the next batch of prompts ∝ uγ (γ≈4 when tasks share skills, 1 on flat pools — see the compounding result below), mixed with a uniform floor (default 10%)—the floor is the anti-forgetting/replay channel and guarantees revisits.
  4. Allocate (optional). Split the rollout budget by greedy water-filling on the marginal mass p̃(1−p̃)N — the probability the next rollout is the group's first success. For fixed supplied pass rates, feasible integer bounds, and a one-step budget, separable concavity makes greedy exact for this proxy.
  5. Train. Practical MaxRL advantages, unchanged within a selected task. Curriculum sampling changes the task distribution, and the drop-both estimator has effective order N−1.

In the repo this is FrontierTeacher + CurriculumSampler: ~150 lines, wired into the official MaxRL/verl trainer behind +data.curriculum.enable=true, with teacher state checkpointed alongside the model — and packaged environment-agnostically in frontier_rl/ for tested local Gym-style tasks and future adapters. Thirty-three native UniLab simulated-manipulation arm runs now validate the exact grouped CPU pipeline, target-preserving importance arithmetic, and a gradient-moment sampler; none establishes a teacher ranking or robotics generality.

The two-line proof (Proposition 1)

Condition on the number of successes K. For K ≥ 1, the MaxRL weights are wsucc = 1/K − 1/N (K of them) and wfail = −1/N (N−K of them), so the total magnitude telescopes:

Σ|w| = K(1K − 1N) + N−KN = 2(1 − KN)   (and 0 when K=0)

Taking expectations with K ~ Binomial(N, p):

𝔼[Σ|w|] = 2·(P(K≥1) − 𝔼[K]/N) = 2·((1−(1−p)N) − p) = 2·(pass@N − pass@1) ∎

The mathematical claims on this page trace to seven propositions with proofs in PROOFS.md (peak location, fixed-p water-filling, RLOO mass ∝ learnability, the frontier mass ratio, the hindsight conditional-law characterization). Core estimator and allocation identities have exact-enumeration/unit tests; experiment-specific checks live in run_validation.py.

Hindsight relabeling — the biggest single gain

MaxRL's Theorem 1 expresses the ML gradient through a success-conditioned score. The practical centered estimator uses failures in live mixed groups, but discards all-fail groups. The HER-style complement: a failed trajectory is a success for the goal it actually reached. Relabeling dead groups to their deepest achieved sub-goal converts wasted compute into live MaxRL groups. This is not generally an exact or unbiased replacement for a fresh target-task group: verifier validity and conditioning rewrite are necessary, and update equality additionally needs equality of the relevant moments (full rewritten-group law equality is sufficient).

Comparing with true-pass-rate proportional priority—and the concentration surprise

Two late results sharpened the method. First, sampling ∝ u(p)γ with γ≈4 beats proportional sampling in the corrected 12-seed skill chain (checkpoint mean 0.781 vs 0.732): the mass functional supplied a useful ordering on this pool, but learning compounds on structured task pools — steps on the highest-mass task unlock the next—so a sharper tested temperature can outperform proportional sampling. An archived two-line ODE pilot was consistent with a shared-skill explanation, but its driver was not retained; the causal explanation remains a hypothesis. A separate retained diagnostic is equally important: the true-pass-rate proportional-priority comparator accumulated 841 units of coefficient mass versus 838 for the pseudo-count teacher, yet their AUCs were 0.851 and 0.700. Similar total coefficient mass did not imply similar AUC; timing, estimation error, and update direction also matter.

In the audited historical GPU maze logs, dense hindsight had the highest reported point estimate in one comparison (final 0.258, best 0.269), while teacher-feedback p̂ inflation was observed. Unequal budgets, RNG coupling, relabel-count scaling, and other audit confounds make both the ranking and the feedback policy provisional pending a corrected rerun.

On the skill chain we can compute the true success-conditioned ML gradient exactly and measure alignment. Centered relabeled groups are strongly direction-aligned, but their mean scale differs by roughly ±6%; cosine alone hid that bias. The hidden benefits — quieter, arguably worth more:

An estimator-derived difficulty scale

ADARFT-style bands need targets and widths tuned per task. Here the band is derived: the rollout budget N you already chose sets the peak (≈ ln N / N) and the width. Larger N moves the coefficient-mass peak toward lower pass rates, although floor, concentration, decay, and task discretization remain empirical choices—and practical N also changes objective order.

A learner-interaction hypothesis

The historical maze evidence raises a testable possibility: if a learner relies on easy prompts for maintenance, a frontier curriculum may amplify coverage loss. The three-seed audited GRPO observation is confounded and hypothesis-generating; a corrected factorial is required before making a general interaction claim.

Conceptual compression

Learnability curricula (p(1−p)), DAPO-style dynamic filtering, and HER stop being three separate tricks: their coefficient-mass scores meet at N=2, filtering changes sampling, and HER adds auxiliary success targets. Fewer moving parts to reason about, without claiming the methods are identical.

Free telemetry

The teacher's pseudo-count estimate is a live difficulty map of the task pool—mastered/frontier/dead fractions per step. Historical GPU diagnostics tracked held-out pass rates, but corrected validation is still required.

7 · Insights we took from the paper

Compute changes the objective, not just the variance

For REINFORCE, more rollouts lower variance on the same objective. For raw/always-CV MaxRL, N sets order N; for practical drop-both MaxRL it sets order N−1. Rollout allocation therefore changes both signal probability and effective objective order.

The zero-gradient zone is structural, not a bug

The raw success-average estimator is order-N unbiased. Dropping the centered control variate at K=0 creates practical order N−1 and a zero update on all-fail groups. A teacher or semantically valid auxiliary target addresses that practical dead zone.

Population curves mislead at finite N

The elegant w(p) table is an infinite-sample statement. Realized coefficient mass is throttled by pass@N for every estimator; on hard prompts GRPO delivers half of what its curve promises. Advantage mass supplies one explicit finite-sample proxy; whether it predicts useful target improvement better than alternatives is the next empirical question.

Existing curricula are special cases

SFL's learnability p(1−p) equals our half-mass utility at N=2 and half RLOO's expected coefficient mass. SEC's empirical-|advantage| reward has these formulas as expectations. Our pseudo-count teacher is a model-based estimate of expected SEC-style mass, not privileged ground-truth scheduling.
