The estimator decides

Public project page — not anonymous. This page links to the authors' public repository and must not be included in a double-blind submission bundle. The two paper PDFs below are the anonymous submission artifacts; this surrounding site is a public research record with historical results and explicit evidence-status notes.

RL with verifiable rewards leans on two data-level fixes — difficulty curricula (train where learning is possible) and failure recycling (relabel failed rollouts as successes of what they actually achieved). Their effects depend on the finite-group estimator underneath. For the practical centered/drop MaxRL implementation, the expected coefficient mass is 2·(pass@N − pass@1), and the expected update factorizes into half that scalar times a task-specific success-versus-failure score separation. Coefficient mass is not a gradient norm, signal-to-noise ratio, transfer guarantee, or performance prediction. Raw MaxRL and full-control-variate MaxRL have different finite-group behavior. In local exact-score controls, full CV updates all-fail groups but does not reliably bootstrap one extreme frontier; verified relabeling changes the data proposal and does. On Countdown, recycling raises mean success while reducing pass@16 coverage. A destination gate has one promising frontier operating point, but is not yet a validated monotone dial. Historical maze runs are descriptive because sampler mix and warm starts are unbalanced.

Evidence at a glance

Finite-group control: across 20 paired exact-score streams, practical MaxRL plus hindsight reaches 0.9269±0.0032 frontier AUC; full CV emits negative-only all-fail updates but remains near zero at the matched budget. This is a local mechanism control, not a universal estimator ranking.  ·  Countdown sharpening: three-seed tier-1 mean@16 changes .278±.066→.324±.014 while pass@16 changes .541±.024→.492±.013 (sample SD).  ·  Maze and Gym: the frozen maze registry supports a descriptive estimator-associated coverage pattern; the Gym controls show that shared transfer can crack sparse binary-reward tasks, while the target-only practical estimator remains empirically frozen.

0 · Why this direction

MaxRL's weight-function view (below) shows the likelihood objective already pours gradient into hard prompts — an implicit curriculum. That raises a question with a non-obvious answer: if the objective already reweights toward hard prompts, is there anything left for a curriculum to do? Either curricula are redundant with likelihood-based RL (a useful negative result), or they do something gradient reweighting cannot (a useful positive one).

The practical centered/drop estimator acts only after a prompt is sampled and is silent on constant-reward groups. A curriculum can reallocate rollout budget but cannot create missing success-versus-failure contrast inside an all-fail group. This statement is implementation-specific: the full-control-variate estimator is a sample-level counterexample, because it emits a negative-only all-fail update. Local activity still does not determine cross-task transfer or final performance, so the page treats the activity profile as a sampling score to test rather than a proof of optimal allocation.

Channel 1 → the teacher (waste avoidance)

For practical centered/drop MaxRL, expected coefficient mass has the closed form 2·(pass@N − pass@1). Its two endpoint zeros and interior peak provide an estimator-aligned sampling score without a hand-set target difficulty. The quantity exactly scales a prompt-local expected update after multiplying a task-specific score separation; it is not itself the update norm or expected improvement.

Channel 2 → the recycler (proposal change)

An exact relabel map can turn failed source trajectories into verified examples for an achieved destination. Verification establishes label validity, not an on-policy destination gradient: destinations are drawn from a source-induced proposal and same-group selection adds dependence. In the 20-seed tabular frontier control, this proposal change succeeds where prompt reallocation and full-CV negative-only updates do not reliably bootstrap.

Implementation scope matters

The paper's proposed contrastive recycler chooses one destination for an all-fail group, rewrites and re-verifies all N trajectories for that destination, and updates only when the rewritten group contains both successes and failures. The historical maze runs instead used per-trajectory positive-only relabeling, and Countdown used per-trace achieved-value targets. Neither historical mechanism instantiates the proposed common-destination contrastive algorithm. They support descriptive recycling and coverage observations, not validation of that algorithm.

Historical maze runs show an estimator-associated coverage separation, but the cohort mixes samplers and reuses warm starts. The GSM8K study is a small treatment-delivery pilot with a replacement confound. Neither identifies a causal curriculum-by-estimator interaction or a broad "safety" result. The full historical decomposition remains available in EVIDENCE.md, with the revised paper providing the claim-status authority.

1 · How MaxRL works, in three steps

Step 1 — RL and maximum likelihood optimize different things. For a prompt x, let p be the model's probability of producing a verified-correct answer (its pass rate). Standard RL maximizes the expected reward E[p]; maximum likelihood maximizes E[log p]. Their gradients differ by one factor:

∇JRL = 𝔼x[ ∇p ]    vs.    ∇JML = 𝔼x[ 1p ∇p ]

That 1/p is the key population weighting difference: maximum likelihood emphasizes hard, low-pass-rate prompts, while plain RL treats every prompt equally. In differentiable supervised learning this is why cross-entropy works so well — but with sampled rollouts and a binary verifier you cannot compute ∇log p directly.

Step 2 — the Maclaurin bridge. The paper's key identity expands log p in failure events, turning the intractable objective into an infinite mixture of pass@k objectives:

log p = −Σk≥1 (1−p)kk   ⇒   ∇JML = Σk≥1 1k ∇pass@k

Plain RL keeps only the k=1 term — it is a first-order approximation of maximum likelihood. Truncating the series at order T gives a family of objectives that interpolates from RL (T=1) to ML (T→∞).

Step 3 — three finite-group conventions. Sample N rollouts and count K successes. Raw MaxRL averages successful score functions and targets truncation order N. The full-control-variate estimator keeps the zero-mean score control, also targets order N, and can emit a negative-only update when K=0. The deployed practical centered/drop convention zeroes constant groups and targets order N−1. Its implementation normalizes by group mean reward and then centers over the requested group. These conventions are not interchangeable, so every claim on this page names the practical convention when that distinction matters.

2 · Where the implicit curriculum goes silent

The weight-function view is a population (infinite-sample) statement. With finite groups of N rollouts, three gaps open up — and they are precisely the gaps a data-level teacher can close:

Dead prompts (K = 0)

If p ≪ 1/N, all N rollouts fail with probability (1−p)N ≈ 1. The group is dropped: zero practical centered/drop update and spent generation compute. Raw MaxRL, GRPO, and RLOO are also silent there; full-control-variate MaxRL is the important exception and emits a negative-only update. Historical maze logs record many all-fail groups, but their exact rate is configuration-specific.

Mastered prompts (p → 1)

∇p → 0, so rollouts can be spent re-confirming what the model already knows. GRPO's population weight grows again near this endpoint, but whether that helps retention or hurts multi-sample coverage is an empirical, task-dependent question.

Uniform rollout budgets

The paper uses a fixed N per prompt. But since the truncation order is tied to the group size (T = N−1 for the practical drop-constant-group estimator), changing N changes both first-success probability and the local objective approximation. The paper proves a fixed-rate one-step allocation result; it does not claim general optimality for adaptive rollout allocation.

At p = 0.01 and N = 32, the finite-group GRPO coefficient mass is roughly half its corresponding population-scale proxy because most groups are all-fail. This is a statement about realized coefficients, not a direct measurement of gradient norm or learning progress. Population curves therefore need a finite-group audit before being used for allocation.

3 · Our proposal: read the curriculum off the estimator

Instead of importing a curriculum heuristic (difficulty bands, learning-progress bandits), we asked: what coefficient mass does the practical MaxRL estimator place on a prompt, in expectation? Define the coefficient mass of a group as the total magnitude of its centered coefficients, Σ|w|. For the practical centered/drop estimator (wsucc = 1/K − 1/N, wfail = −1/N, group dropped at K=0), the algebra collapses to something exact and interpretable:

𝔼[ Σ|w| ] = 2·( pass@N(p) − pass@1(p) ) = 2·( (1−(1−p)N) − p )

The expected coefficient mass is twice the probability that a prompt is solved within N attempts but not within one. More precisely, the expected update equals half this mass times the prompt's success-versus-failure score separation. The mass vanishes at both endpoints and peaks at

p★ = 1 − N−1/(N−1) ≈ ln NN

Larger group sizes shift this estimator-activity peak toward lower pass rates. We use that profile as a sampler score; the peak is not, by itself, a proof of the downstream-optimal curriculum band.

Watch it work: the frontier walk, animated

The teacher loop

  1. Track. Keep a decayed Beta(α, β) posterior over each prompt's pass rate, updated from every requested group's observed successes. The historical default decay 0.7 is a tuned tracking choice, not a derived constant.
  2. Score. Thompson-sample p̃ from each posterior; compute u(p̃) = (1−(1−p̃)N) − p̃. Sampling from the posterior gives optimism on rarely-visited prompts for free.
  3. Sample. Draw the next batch of prompts ∝ uγ, mixed with a uniform floor. Historical defaults (γ=1 or 4 and a 10% floor) are tuned knobs; γ=4 helped one chain control but did not transfer reliably to the maze.
  4. Allocate (optional). Split the rollout budget by greedy water-filling on the marginal mass p̃(1−p̃)N — the probability the next rollout is the group's first success. Greedy water-filling solves the paper's separable fixed-rate one-step problem; it is not a general sequential-allocation theorem.
  5. Train. Apply the explicitly selected estimator convention. The reference framework defaults to practical centered/drop MaxRL; raw and full-CV variants have different finite-group contracts.

In the repo this is FrontierTeacher + CurriculumSampler: ~150 lines, wired into the official MaxRL/verl trainer behind +data.curriculum.enable=true, with teacher state checkpointed alongside the model — and packaged environment-agnostically in frontier_rl/ for gym and robotics tasks.

The two-line proof (Proposition 1)

Condition on the number of successes K. For K ≥ 1, the MaxRL weights are wsucc = 1/K − 1/N (K of them) and wfail = −1/N (N−K of them), so the total magnitude telescopes:

Σ|w| = K(1K − 1N) + N−KN = 2(1 − KN)   (and 0 when K=0)

Taking expectations with K ~ Binomial(N, p):

𝔼[Σ|w|] = 2·(P(K≥1) − 𝔼[K]/N) = 2·((1−(1−p)N) − p) = 2·(pass@N − pass@1) ∎

Derivations and legacy validation notes are collected in PROOFS.md (peak location, the bounded one-step allocation problem, estimator comparisons, and the hindsight conditional-law characterization), with numerical checks in run_validation.py. Those identities establish coefficient and conditional-law statements; empirical transfer and performance claims require separate experiments.

Rewriting the conditioning and re-running an exact verifier establishes semantic label validity. What the registered run showed — and the replication

Registered-run diagnostic: grpo+teacher ended below plain grpo (mean@4 −.027; pass@4 −.048) — the oft-quoted second-half dip .096→.093 is itself within noise and carries no weight alone. What we learned beyond the predictions

The run averages only ~0.4 group visits per prompt, so a prompt-level posterior has little evidence and the resulting sampler remains near uniform. External-difficulty and posterior- agreement diagnostics require non-vendored caches and are therefore not paper evidence.

What the pilot cannot identify

Token entropy, pass@k, and mean accuracy can move differently, but the available two-seed pilot cannot attribute those movements to estimator geometry, the curriculum, or prompt replacement. A frozen evaluation harness and generated-token accounting are also required.

E-LLM-2 result — two honest nulls, one new phenomenon

The Countdown implementation assigned each failed trace its own achieved-value target and rewrote that trace's prompt and response before exact verification. This per-trace-target mechanism is not the paper's proposed one-destination-per-group contrastive recycler. The first pool was too easy: the uniform baseline reached 94/76/94% of its random-guesser bound, so the registered allocation and recycling improvements were null. The pool was rebuilt with operand permutation, parenthesization, exact division, and a headroom screen before the three-seed sharpening comparison below.

The finding: recycling-induced sharpening (3 seeds)

On the rebuilt real-structure pool, recycling lifts tier-1 mean@16 by +.046 (.278±.066 → .324±.014) while losing .049 of pass@16 coverage (.541±.024 → .492±.013), reported as sample SD across three training seeds. On frontier tier 2, mean changes .117±.037→.143±.017 while coverage changes .274±.018→.237±.079. Exact verification preserves labels but not destination balance; source-policy occupancy can replay already-reachable outcomes more often. This is a measured mean-versus-coverage trade-off, not proof of a universal mechanism.

Destination saturation is promising, not yet a validated dial

At the unintended three-seed moderate gate setting, frontier-tier coverage is .279±.024 versus baseline .274±.018, while mean@16 is .133±.008 and retains roughly 60% of the ungated mean-increment point estimate. On saturated tier 1, the same setting is .282±.037 mean / .484±.014 coverage and does not restore baseline coverage. A decay-math bug made this operating point weaker than intended. The corrected stronger setting has only one seed (.220/.564 mean/coverage on tier 1; .083/.238 on tier 2), so the available runs do not establish monotonicity, noninferiority, or a Pareto frontier. The estimator, sampler, and relabeling contracts are packaged as an environment-agnostic reference framework (frontier_rl/, numpy-only core). You implement four methods — task bins, group rollout, hindsight relabel, and one weighted policy update. Boundary control: binary-success reward on classic control (3 seeds)

Setting, stated precisely: all arms train on binary task success (reach x ≥ x*; survive ≥ T) with the same group estimator — the classic-control analogue of a verifier reward, not the dense native rewards standard libraries (SB3 etc.) train on. Under sparse reward, the target-only practical centered/drop arm remains empirically frozen in the committed runs: no hardest-task success is observed, and any all-fail group receives zero practical-estimator weights. Raw MaxRL, GRPO, and RLOO are also silent on an all-fail group; full-control-variate MaxRL is the exception and can emit a negative-only update. The artifacts do not record every parameter update, so this empirical freeze is not a claim about every possible budget or exploration mechanism. Honesty about the dense world: with CartPole's native +1/step reward, plain REINFORCE on the same tile policy solves survive-400 (0.963±0.038, 3 seeds) — the categorical zero is a property of this sparse verifier-style setting, making it a useful RLVR boundary control. MountainCar's native reward (−1/step, constant until first success) flatlines policy gradients either way — the hard-exploration classic.

Three-rung attribution at a common 280-step horizon (hard-task AUC): uniform-over-bins spread gets most of the way (MC .750 → +recycling .895 → +teacher .956; CP .708 → .792 → .893; teacher increment positive 3/3 seeds both envs); creation-without-allocation is below ceiling on CartPole (0.958 every seed) while adding the teacher reaches 1.000. The teacher is 3.0× faster to hard-task 0.9 in this control. Final gated-stack performance is 1.000±0.000 on both official tasks; the paper treats these as Gym boundary results, not as evidence that one schedule transfers unchanged to every domain. Methods note: our first version of this study silently used per-bin policy parameters and reproduced the known failure (0.000 everywhere) — restoring a shared-policy design is what cracks the task. In this control, transfer required shared policy parameters.

MountainCar is a deliberate stress test: sparse flag-success is a classic hard-exploration task and an analogue of the tested frontier-heavy exact-score regime, where several practical samplers remain near zero at the matched budget. Its curriculum ("reach x ≥ x*, walking x* from the valley to the flag") is the template for robotics reach tasks: swap position bins for end-effector distance bands and the adapter carries over. (The earlier demo-budget 5-arm table was measured on the superseded per-bin configuration and has been retired; the convergence study above is the citable result.)

Real training curves — performance rising while the curriculum walks

Case study: shared transfer is a boundary condition

The three-seed plateau control uses one shared, goal-conditioned policy and reaches the MountainCar flag under a graded curriculum, while the target-only practical estimator remains frozen. An earlier transition-matched checkpoint reported larger 10-seed differences, but its raw artifact is not vendored and it is therefore withdrawn as paper evidence. The hidden benefits — quieter, arguably worth more:

Zero difficulty hyperparameters

ADARFT-style bands need targets and widths tuned per task. Here the band is derived: the rollout budget N sets the coefficient-mass peak and width, avoiding a hand-set target pass rate. The full sampler still has tuned decay, floor, and concentration knobs, and changing N does not guarantee better downstream performance.

A coverage diagnostic, not a safety theorem

Estimator geometry motivates reporting pass@k alongside mean accuracy and auditing which tasks receive updates. The historical maze sign separation is descriptive; a balanced schedule-replay factorial is still required to test a curriculum-by-estimator interaction.

Conceptual compression

The N=2 coefficient-mass slice connects exactly to p(1−p), while dynamic filtering and hindsight act at different points in the data pipeline. The connection clarifies interfaces; it does not make the methods equivalent or eliminate their separate tuning and proposal bias.

Free telemetry

The teacher posterior provides a live map of requested-task evidence. It can also expose posterior inflation when relabeled outcomes are fed back to the wrong task. Historical truth-agreement diagnostics require non-vendored caches and are not used as paper evidence.

7 · Insights we took from the paper

Compute changes the objective, not just the variance

For REINFORCE, more rollouts = lower variance on the same objective. For MaxRL, more rollouts change the truncation order of the fixed raw/full-CV and practical conventions (N and N−1, respectively). Adaptive per-task truncation is not validated here, and the historical adaptive-T result is withdrawn.

The zero-gradient zone is structural, not a bug

The practical centered/drop convention is silent on K=0. This does not imply that every MaxRL estimator is silent: the full control variate emits a negative-only update. Local controls test both that estimator-side alternative and a data-proposal change through verified hindsight.

Population curves mislead at finite N

The w(p) table is a population statement. Constant-group events and implementation normalizers change finite-N coefficient mass. That mass is useful for allocation, but it is not a gradient norm, SNR, transfer effect, or guarantee of improvement.

Existing curricula are special cases

SFL's learnability p(1−p) equals normalized practical-MaxRL coefficient mass at N=2 and is proportional to RLOO mass under the stated convention. For N>2 the profile is group-size- and estimator-specific. This algebraic overlap does not subsume the broader methods.

Abstract (current draft)

Difficulty curricula and hindsight relabeling are often treated as objective-agnostic modules for reinforcement learning with verifiable binary rewards. We show that their effect depends on the finite-group estimator underneath. For the practical centered MaxRL implementation that drops all-fail groups, a prompt with pass rate p and N rollouts has expected coefficient mass AN(p)=2[(1−p)−(1−p)N]. Its expected update factorizes as AN(p)/2 times the success-versus-failure score separation, so the normalized mass is the estimator-controlled scalar multiplying that separation. Its shape and unique peak are set by N, motivating a prompt sampler without a hand-set target difficulty. The result is implementation-specific: raw and full-control-variate MaxRL target order N, while the deployed drop-all-fail estimator targets N−1; the full variant assigns negative coefficients when every rollout fails. In a matched-budget 20-seed tabular control, that alternative invokes the update callback for every all-fail group but does not reliably bootstrap the frontier (its median rounds to zero), while verified hindsight reaches 0.927±0.003 AUC. Relabeling produces a verified destination-conditioned update under a source-induced proposal, not generally an on-policy destination gradient. In historical Countdown runs, a legacy per-trace relabeler raises mean success while reducing pass@16 coverage across three seeds; its saturation gate has one promising but unintended moderate operating point. Historical maze runs use a legacy utility and isolated positive imitation updates. They show an estimator-associated coverage separation, but neither neural suite instantiates the proposed common-destination algorithm, and the maze's unbalanced sampler mix and shared warm starts preclude a causal interaction claim. Together the theory and experiments delimit what allocation can change, what relabeling can create, and why both mean success and pass@k coverage must be reported.
